Hours a year = hours in one cycle × 365 ÷ days in the cycle. The exact number in a given year changes by a few shifts depending on where your crew starts. For comparison, a regular 40-hour week is 2,080 hours a year (40 × 52 weeks), and three 12-hour shifts a week (36 hours) are 1,872.

Shift differential and overtime: a worked week

Night and evening differentials are part of the regular rate, so they raise your overtime pay (29 CFR 778.207(b) and 778.109). Take a week with three 12-hour nights and one 12-hour day, $25 an hour and a $3 night differential:

Straight-time pay48 h × $25 + 36 night h × $3$1,308
Regular rate$1,308 ÷ 48 h$27.25 an hour
Overtime premium8 h over 40 × half of $27.25$109
Total for the week$1,417

Working the premium out on the base rate alone ($12.50 × 8 = $100) would pay $9 too little that week. Enter your night differential above and the calculator does this for every week of the year.

What is a shift differential?

A shift differential is extra pay for working less popular hours: evenings, nights, weekends or holidays. It is usually a fixed amount for each of those hours, such as $2 more an hour on nights, or a percentage of your base rate. Federal law does not require it: the FLSA "does not require extra pay for night work" (US Department of Labor). The amount comes from your employer's policy or your union contract. Federal employees get 10% for regular night work (5 U.S.C. 5545(a)); shift hours and differentials has more benchmarks.

Does shift differential apply to overtime and PTO?

To overtime, yes: it is part of the regular rate (29 CFR 778.207(b)), as the worked week above shows. PTO is different. Vacation, holiday and sick pay are not pay for hours worked (29 CFR 778.218), so whether your PTO includes the differential depends on your employer's policy or contract.

Can a shift differential be taken away?

Usually, for shifts you have not worked yet. No federal law requires a differential, so an employer can change or end it unless a union contract or written agreement guarantees it.

Overtime after 8 or 12 hours: federal and state rules

Federal law does not require overtime for long days, only for long weeks: there is no overtime for hours over 8 in a day as such (29 CFR 778.102). It pays time and a half after 40 hours in the workweek (29 CFR 778.105), on the regular rate, which includes night differentials (29 CFR 778.109). California also pays after 8 hours in a day and double after 12 (Labor Code 510); health care employers can adopt 12-hour alternative workweeks (IWC Wage Order 5). Alaska pays after 8 hours a day (AS 23.10.060) and Colorado after 12 hours in a workday or 12 hours in a row (COMPS Order #40). Fire and police departments often use a 7(k) work period instead: see the 7(k) calculator. If you work in another state, check its labor department: a few states have their own rules, and a union contract can also pay daily overtime.

Questions

Which states pay overtime after 8 hours a day?

California pays time and a half after 8 hours in a workday and double time after 12 (Labor Code 510), and Alaska after 8 hours in a day (AS 23.10.060, with exemptions such as hospital employees who provide medical services). Colorado pays after 12 hours in a workday or 12 hours in a row (COMPS Order #40). Federal law only pays overtime after 40 hours in the workweek (29 CFR 778.102 and 778.105).

How much does a 12-hour Pitman schedule pay a year?

A Pitman schedule works about 2,190 hours a year, or 42 hours a week on average. At $25 an hour that is about $54,750 before overtime; with federal overtime on the weeks over 40 hours, a little more.

How many hours a year is a 48/96 schedule?

About 2,920 hours: 48 hours on and 96 off is an average of 56 hours a week.

Is overtime paid on a 24/48 schedule?

Firefighters at public departments are usually paid under the FLSA 7(k) exemption, with overtime after 212 hours in 28 days. Private employers pay overtime after 40 hours a week.
